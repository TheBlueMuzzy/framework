# Creative game-design Discover — what designers actually do before the GDD

Research for F217 (the discover pipeline). 2026-11-09. Builds on `discover-research.md` (Double Diamond heuristics H1–H26: divergent thinking, defer judgement, analogous inspiration, extremes, triangulation, hopes and fears). This file covers what that one left out: **the game-design craft of the early stage**, meaning how designers find the experience, theme, verbs, nouns, mechanisms and prior art that a GDD is later written from.

**How first-hand this is:**
- **Fetched:** every URL in Sources marked (F) was downloaded in full (HTTP 200) and its text read. Quotes from these pages are exact.
- **Snippet:** URLs marked (S) were seen only in web-search excerpts, because the site refused automated fetches (BoardGameGeek 403, MobyGames Cloudflare challenge, Clint Hocking's blog 403, GDC schedule 404) or no fetch was tried. Claims resting on them are marked **(snippet)** inline.
- **Second-hand:** Schell's lens questions come from Ted Neward's reading notes on *The Art of Game Design* (F), not the book itself. Rosenberg's Agricola quotes come through League of Gamemakers (F). Anthropy and Clark come through a review in First Person Scholar (F). None of the books were read directly.
- Where a claim is my own reasoning and not a source's, it says **extrapolated**.

Muzzy's seed, which this research extrapolates from: Discover helps write the GDD. It steps through ideation and researches whether the idea already exists, which mechanisms might fit, and which themes the idea serves. It breaks the topic down into simulation thinking: what verbs and nouns the theme has. Define then turns those into a 3–5 line experience story, the verbs into mechanisms and the nouns into interactables. "Discover collects the bricks; Define chooses which bricks to build with."

## 1. Ideation methods: what each says the early stage is for, and its concrete moves

### Tracy Fullerton — *Game Design Workshop* (playcentric design)
- **What the early stage is for:** set **player experience goals** before any features. "These are not features of the game but rather descriptions of the interesting and unique situations in which you hope players will find themselves." Her examples: "players will have to cooperate to win, but the game will be structured so they can never trust each other"; "players will feel a sense of happiness and playfulness rather than competitiveness." — [Game Developer excerpt, 5th ed.](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/book-excerpt-game-design-workshop) (F)
- Experience goals come **during** brainstorming: "Setting player experience goals up front, as a part of your brainstorming process, can also focus your creative process … Features will be brainstormed later to meet these goals." — same (F)
- **Moves/questions:** think about "what experiences they will describe to their friends later"; "What are they thinking as they make choices in your game? How are they feeling? Are the choices you've offered as rich and interesting as they can be?" — same (F)
- Prototype right after brainstorming: "construct a playable version of their idea immediately after brainstorming ideas … a physical prototype of the core game mechanics." — same (F)
- The 5th edition adds "emotion-focused experience goals". — [Routledge 5th ed. page](https://www.routledge.com/link/link/p/book/9781032607016) (S, snippet)

### Jesse Schell — *The Art of Game Design* (lenses)
All quotes are from [Ted Neward's reading notes](https://research.tedneward.com/gamedev/art-of-game-design.html) (F, second-hand).
- **Lens #1 Essential Experience:** "What experience do I want the player to have? What is essential to that experience? How can my game capture that essence?"
- **Lens #4 Curiosity:** "What questions does my game put into the player's mind? What am I doing to make them care about these questions?"
- **Lens #6 Problem Solving:** "What problems does my game ask the player to solve? Are there hidden problems to solve that arise as part of gameplay?"
- **Lens #7 Elemental Tetrad:** a game is made of four elements, Mechanics, Story, Aesthetics and Technology. "Are the four elements in harmony, reinforcing each other, and working together toward a common theme?"
- **Lens #9 Unification (theme):** "What is my theme? Am I using every means possible to reinforce the theme?" "Some themes are better than others because they resonate with people more closely."
- **Lens #10 Resonance:** "What is it about my game that feels powerful and special? When I describe my game to people, what ideas get them really excited? If I had no constraints of any kind, what would this game be like? I have certain instincts about how this game should be. What is driving those instincts?"
- **Lens #11 Infinite Inspiration:** "Stop looking at your game, and stop looking at games like it. Instead, look everywhere else. What is an experience I have had in my life that I would want to share with others?"
- **Lens #12 Problem Statement:** "Think of your game as the solution to a problem." (This is the only lens close to the corporate framing Muzzy rejected. Here the "problem" is the design's own goals and limits, not a user's pain.)
- **Lens #15 Toy:** "Stop thinking about whether your game is fun to play, and start thinking about whether it is fun to play with … If my game had no goal, would it be fun at all? … When people see my game, do they want to start interacting with it, even before they know what to do?" The "braver way" is "to invent and create new toys before you even have any idea what games will be played with them."
- **Brainstorm tips:** "Write them (all) down", "Number Your Lists", "Toys", "Change Your Perspective", "Immerse Yourself", and **"Mix and Match Categories"**: separate idea lists for Technology, Mechanics, Story and Aesthetics, then combine across them.
- **Lens #13 Eight Filters:** filters include "Innovation. Is this game novel enough?" These are judging filters, so they belong to **Define**, not Discover.

### MDA — Hunicke, LeBlanc, Zubek (2004)
- Eight aesthetics, used as an experience vocabulary: "Sensation: Game as sense-pleasure · Fantasy: make-believe · Narrative: drama · Challenge: obstacle course · Fellowship: social framework · Discovery: uncharted territory · Expression: self-discovery · Submission: pastime". "Each game pursues multiple aesthetic goals, in varying degrees." — [MDA paper PDF](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf) (F; the earlier research could not read it, this run could)
- Dynamics produce aesthetics: "challenge is created by things like time pressure and opponent play." — same (F)

### Sid Meier — "Games are a series of interesting decisions" (GDC 2012)
- A warning about mashup ideation: "designs are kind of about putting together pieces of other games … if some games are fun, then combinations of their elements will also be fun. 'Unfortunately, that doesn't always work out' … a more useful way to look at a new game design [is] in terms of, what are the decisions I'm presenting the player, and are they interesting?" — [Game Developer report](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions) (F)
- Interesting decisions have tradeoffs, are situational, express play style, persist, and are informed. Decision types: risk vs reward, short vs long term, customisation. — same (F)
- **Theme brings knowledge for free:** "if you're building a game about railroads or pirates, there's a lot that the player can bring to a topic like that that they already know." Zombies work because "their motivation is basic and their nature is obvious." — same (F)
- Quality equals fantasy plus decisions: "It's the combination of this wonderful fantasy world that you create and the interesting decisions that the player gets to make in that world." — same (F)

### Will Wright — subject research, toys and caricature
- His method starts from an obsession with a subject, then steps back from it: "What is the basis of my obsession? What is it that I think is really cool about this?" I try to figure out how I can communicate that to other players in a game. Basically, how does the game become a caricature of that subject … It becomes a toy." — [AV Club interview](https://www.avclub.com/will-wright-1798208435) (F)
- Real research behind the toys: SimCity applied Jay Forrester's urban-dynamics theories; The Sims came from his ant-colony research for SimAnt plus Christopher Alexander's pattern work. He wanted SimCity to feel "more like Legos to me than a standard game." — [MIT Technology Review, 2005](https://www.technologyreview.com/2005/04/01/231307/life-is-a-game/) (F)
- "Possibility space" as his term for what simple rules let players do. — [Wikipedia, Will Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_Wright_(game_designer)) (S, snippet). The same idea is defined as "the set of everything the player can do; rules define and limit it" on [game-design-cards](https://game-design-cards.pages.dev/) (F).

### Toy first / "find the fun"
- "You should make the toy first, and the game later. Basically, you prototype the core interaction. Not as a game, but more as just a sensual experience." The writer credits the idea, unsure of the source, to "maybe … Chaim Gingold, or … Will Wright." — [Tiny Subversions, "Toys Before Games"](https://www.tinysubversions.com/2009/02/toys-before-games/index.html) (F)
- **Disagreement, in the same page's comments:** "I've actually had mixed results with building the toys first … sometimes it would lead to fun toys without any suggestion of goals," and "testing your toy in its pure state can lead to false positives of fun." — same (F)
- Dan Cook: "A toy has been described as a simple fun activity that 'feels good' … an environment that allows players to create their own goals"; score is "meta game mechanics layered ontop of simple tactile mechanics." — [Lost Garden, 2005](https://lostgarden.com/2005/10/28/article-how-to-prototype-a-game-in-under-7-days-on-gamasutra/) (F)
- How fast does "fun" show? Colin Northway asked 8 successful designers, and "5.5 out of 8 replies were 'it's fun right away'". Others: "the actual prototypes were not enjoyable for months." Vlambeer: "most games come together within a few days." — [Northway Games](https://northwaygames.com/how-does-a-good-game-start/) (F)

### Steve Swink — *Game Feel*
- Game feel is "a Venn diagram of real-time response, simulated space, and polish". Real-time means a correction cycle "of under 100 milliseconds". — [Liz England's review](https://lizengland.com/blog/review-game-feel-by-steve-swink/) (F)
- Swink on timing: "From the beginning of preproduction until the final game ships, design should include game feel"; "Game feel needs prototyping too." — [Game Developer, "Game Feel: The Secret Ingredient"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-feel-the-secret-ingredient) (F, via a fetch helper's report)
- Its limit: useful "for games like platformers, action-adventure, shooters, and racing games, but not so much if your work doesn't rely on timed reactions or player movement." — same (F). Researchers also call the definition too narrow (S, snippet, via [Wikipedia, Game feel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_feel)).
- What it means for Discover (extrapolated): for real-time games, the feel of the core verb is a question you can only answer by playing it. It goes to `/sketch` as a probe, not to reading.

### Theme-first vs mechanic-first (board-game writing)
- **Theme-first:** Seth Jaffee: "I often have a main mechanism in mind, I look for a theme to use it with, and then I let the theme direct how the rest of the game shapes up … Even if you start with a main mechanism, it's best to find a theme early … it makes for a lot more thematic consonance." — [Jaffee's blog](https://sedjtroll.blogspot.com/2013/12/theme-first-vs-mechanics-first-design.html) (F)
- Uwe Rosenberg on Agricola, quoted second-hand: "I thought about a theme which justified a controlled and limited increase of your workers … to conceive children as a couple needs time," and "I'm always reading about the theme." — [League of Gamemakers](https://leagueofgamemakers.com/theme-is-more-important-than-mechanics) (F)
- **Vision-first:** "it doesn't matter where you start as long as you end up with theme and mechanics that are in strong alignment … the vision for the game is a better starting point." — [Game Developer, marry theme and mechanics](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/how-to-marry-the-theme-and-mechanics-in-your-board-game) (F)
- **Neither first:** "neither comes first, but they evolve with each other" (a commenter); "theme tends to be metaphor: a way to explain the machinations of the rules." — comments on [Jesper Juul, "Should game mechanics match the game theme?"](https://www.jesperjuul.net/ludologist/2004/08/11/should-game-mechanics-match-the-game-theme-aka-rules-and-fiction/) (F)
- **Mechanic-first, by catalogue:** "Go over to Boardgamegeek and check out their list of game mechanics. Pick one and begin. Actually, you should pick two." — League of Gamemakers (F). Muzzy's view is that this list is a poor source; see section 3.

### Jam and quick-ideation techniques
- **Constraints as fuel:** "By pondering on how to make the game intriguing within these constraints, one can expect to come up with ideas distinct from previous ones." Constraint types: interaction ("only two buttons"), visuals, unusual goals ("destroy oneself", "utilize enemies to win"). "Overly stringent constraints can make overcoming them arduous." — [abagames, Restrictions](https://abagames.github.io/joys-of-small-game-development-en/restrictions) (F)
- **Mashups, SCAMPER, other media:** "Merge seemingly odd elements together, such as card games + fishing"; "Employ the SCAMPER method on existing games"; "Turn your attention to other media"; "Leverage your own experiences." — [abagames, Ideas](https://abagames.github.io/joys-of-small-game-development-en/ideas) (F)
- **Look past the obvious:** "the first five ideas are probably too obvious, and a lot of other participants will implement them anyway." Add a self-constraint: in a shoot-'em-up, "the player can't shoot". "Try to play the game in your head." — [Game Developer, jam ideas](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/coming-up-with-ideas-for-a-game-jam-game) (F)
- **Verbs–Nouns–Adjectives (VNA) card game:** an academic ideation game. "Well-suited to blue-sky ideation, but it is harder to use the ideas in ongoing design processes"; "the theme-tuned VNA variant performed best both in theme-related and interesting ideas." — [Kultima & Alha, DiGRA 2011](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/view/583) (F, abstract)

### Where these sources disagree
- **Experience first or verbs first?** Fullerton says set experience goals before features. The IVW framework calls "designing top-down from the experience, as opposed to designing bottom-up through the actions" a trap, and wants verbs defined early "to anchor" the fantasy ([IVW](https://kebaux.bearblog.dev/ivw-game-ideation), F). Cornell's course allows either: "Some people like to begin with verb brainstorming … Others need to understand all the goals and challenges before they understand the verbs" ([Cornell CS 3152](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs3152/2025sp/labs/comm2), F). **For BMUZ (extrapolated):** Discover collects both and doesn't order them; Define checks they agree.
- **Mashups:** abagames recommends them; Meier warns they "don't always work out" unless the decisions are interesting.
- **Toy first:** recommended by Schell, Cook and Tiny Subversions; the comments warn of fun toys with no goals and "false positives of fun".
- **Theme:** theme-first (Jaffee, Rosenberg), vision-first (Game Developer), co-evolving (Juul commenters). Nobody argues for mechanics-only as better design; abstracts are defended as fine, not superior.

## 2. Prior art: does it already exist, and what then?

### How designers check
- **Steam tags:** the Tag Wizard sorts tags into "Genres, Visuals & View Points, Themes & Moods, Features, Player support." Tags are applied by developers, players and moderators, and drive "Browse genres & categories" and "tag-driven recommendations". Its example is a genre ladder: "Super-Genre: Action · Genre: Platformer · Sub-Genre: Precision Platformer". — [Steamworks docs, Tags](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/tags) (F). **Use:** describe the idea as a tag profile, then find games sharing that profile.
- **BoardGameGeek game pages:** useful for prior art ("does a game like this exist?"), not for its mechanics list. See section 3. The site refused automated fetches (403).
- **MobyGames:** refused the fetch (Cloudflare). Its grouping by genre, perspective, setting and so on is known only from its site structure, not verified here: [MobyGames](https://www.mobygames.com/) (S).
- **Analysing other games:** "Analyze other games and ponder their development process. If it were you, how would you make it different?"; "Play legendary games from the past." — [abagames, Ideas](https://abagames.github.io/joys-of-small-game-development-en/ideas) (F)
- **A real design doc doing it:** Warren Spector's Deus Ex proposal has a "Competitive Analysis" section and positions the game by gaps: "we have fantasy covered with Underworld … we have the real world covered with... well, we don't … I can't believe no one's done this before." — [Deus Ex postmortem, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/postmortem-ion-storm-s-i-deus-ex-i-) (F)

### What to do when it exists
- **Existing isn't failure; familiarity sells.** Chris Zukowski: buyers "rarely cite the hook as the reason why they bought the game … They usually said 'because I like the genre'". So pair a **hook** (what makes it unique) with an **anchor** (what makes it "familiar" or "safe"). — [How To Market A Game, "Know your game's anchor"](https://howtomarketagame.com/2019/12/23/know-your-games-anchor/) (F)
- "The Hook is the novel … the 'wow!' factor"; "the Anchor is the expected. The familiar." A hook can be gameplay (Nemesis System), art (Spinch) or narrative (Mutazione). — [Game Developer, Hooks and Anchors](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/indie-insights-hooks-and-anchors) (F)
- **Deliberate differentiation moves:** SCAMPER on the existing game (substitute, combine, adapt, modify, put to other use, eliminate, reverse) and "strip it down": "start with a genre and 'strip it down', removing actions so that you get a smaller mini game … Help the Hero takes an RPG and removes everything except for inventory management." — [abagames](https://abagames.github.io/joys-of-small-game-development-en/ideas) (F); [Cornell CS 3152](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs3152/2025sp/labs/comm2) (F)
- **Lean on the genre for the parts that aren't new:** "If your actions are those from a recognized genre, feel free to say that (e.g. 'our game uses standard platformer movement') … focus on the different elements of your game." — Cornell (F)
- **Extrapolated for BMUZ:** the prior-art step collects, for each close match: what it is, which of our bricks it shares, what players praise, what they complain about or miss, and the hook/anchor it uses. "What did they miss" comes from player complaints (reviews, forums). This report found no designer source that names that exact move, so it is my proposal.

## 3. Mechanism fit: catalogues and how a verb finds its mechanism

### Catalogues
- **Engelstein & Shalev, *Building Blocks of Tabletop Game Design*:** groups mechanisms into categories; "each category has between eight and 26 variations. Each one is named, numbered, and discussed," e.g. "18 different types of auctions". Class exercise: "students are given random patterns and told to design a game using them." — [Deane Barker review](https://deanebarker.net/library/titles/building-blocks-game-design/) (F). Category names (Game Structure, Turn Order, Actions, Resolution, Game End and Victory, Uncertainty, Economics, Auctions, Worker Placement, Movement, Area Control, Set Collection, Card Mechanisms) are **(snippet)** from catalogue records such as [Taylor & Francis](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/mono/10.1201/9780429430701/building-blocks-tabletop-game-design-geoffrey-engelstein-isaac-shalev) (S).
- **Björk & Holopainen, Gameplay Design Patterns:** "descriptions of reoccurring interaction relevant to game play" ([DiGRA 2003 paper](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/view/60), F). The wiki aims "to make it easier to express clearly intentions, analyses, and opinions regarding gameplay" ([gameplaydesignpatterns.org](http://www.gameplaydesignpatterns.org/), F). Its size, about 600 patterns, is **(snippet)** ([Notion guide](https://gamedesigntools.notion.site/Game-Design-Patterns-c98251a4dc684ae680dadeccee195fc6), S).
- **Not a catalogue: BGG's mechanics list** (private vocabulary for Claude, never a resource for the creative: not all entries are mechanics, duplicates, uneven granularity, wargame-heavy). Muzzy, an experienced designer, relayed via the team lead on 2026-11-09, says it is known among experienced designers as a poor taxonomy. Its known problems: "they aren't all mechanics" (non-mechanics are mixed in), some entries are the same thing twice, granularity is uneven ("weirdly granular and weirdly loose"), and it is wargame-heavy. Koster says something similar: many BGG mechanics "are basically composites of these smaller, more a[tomic]" pieces ([Koster](https://www.raphkoster.com/?p=32715), F). **Rule for BMUZ:** Claude may use BGG entries *privately* as vocabulary (what an entry means, how it's built, nearby alternatives, how to think around it). It never shows the list to the creative as a resource, taxonomy or authority. Mechanism fit leans on designer-written sources instead: Building Blocks, the Chalmers patterns wiki, Koster's grammar, Anthropy & Clark's verbs and objects, and designers' own breakdowns of specific games.
- **Genres as verb bundles:** "A game genre is a set of actions and challenges that are common to games of that type." — Cornell (F)

### Matching a verb to a mechanism
- **Describe the verb by its outcome, not its input or skin.** "'Use an item' is never a core verb … 'hurt an enemy' or 'knock back an enemy' are both much more specific than 'shoot an enemy' … a verb should correspond to a single outcome." Skin is separate: "Whether you are attacking with a gun, a magic wand, a bow-and-arrow, or throwing stars, the attacks all create projectiles that cause damage." — [Cornell CS 3152](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs3152/2025sp/labs/comm2) (F). **Extrapolated:** this is the step that makes catalogue lookup work. An outcome verb ("claim a space before others", "trade one thing for another") matches catalogue entries (Area Control, Trading) far better than a themed verb ("stitch").
- **Atoms and patterns:** "Design patterns are basically atom aggregations that we have decided to label." Koster diagrams games "in the form of verbs and the choice trees players can engage in." He uses "the lens of typical problems to build mechanics out of … going straight to atoms or topology to leverage them as fun core mechanics," e.g. a "Hex Blokus, a vertex cover problem." — [Koster, Tabletop Game Grammar](https://www.raphkoster.com/?p=32715) (F)
- **Skill atoms (Dan Cook):** each mechanic as a loop: action → simulation → feedback → the player's mental model, i.e. how the player learns a new skill. Example: Mario's jump. — [Cook, "The Chemistry of Game Design"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-chemistry-of-game-design) (F). **Use:** for each candidate mechanism, ask what skill the player learns from it.
- **The theme can pick the mechanism:** Rosenberg chose a theme that "justified a controlled and limited increase of your workers" (via League of Gamemakers, F). Jaffee found a theme that "could support 4 different aspects" (Conquer, Explore, Discover, Culture) for his 16-card mechanism (F). Matching runs both ways.
- **Decisions check:** every candidate mechanism should give a decision with a tradeoff (Meier, F).

## 4. Theme: finding it, deepening it, joining it to mechanics

### What a theme brings (compiled from several sources)
- **A fantasy / dream:** "What dream are you satisfying? … many games are about wish fulfillment … a response to a statement of the form 'I want to __'". This can be a role ("a crime-fighting zombie"), an activity ("explore a land where I do not know the language") or an emotion ("lonely, but content"), and emotions are "by far, the hardest". — [Cornell](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs3152/2025sp/labs/comm2) (F)
- **The core fantasy:** "the central fantasy the game promises to deliver ('you're a silent ninja', 'build and run an empire') … If a feature doesn't fit the core fantasy, it's often scope you don't need." — [game-design-cards](https://game-design-cards.pages.dev/) (F)
- **Player knowledge for free:** Meier's pirates, railroads, zombies (F).
- **Verbs:** IVW's seamstress. Picking "Thread, Stitch, Seam" makes you a hands-on seamstress; "Manage, Barter, Supply" makes you "a bustling entrepreneur"; "Lick, Zap, Weld" keeps the setting but gives "a curiously different skill set." Same theme, different game. — [IVW](https://kebaux.bearblog.dev/ivw-game-ideation) (F)
- **Entities with motivation and conflict:** "wherever there is meaning, there is also motivation". For each entity: Motivation ("what is the entity's goal?") and Conflict ("what is … preventing the entity from achieving their goal?"). This "helps us avoid worldbuilding through thematic embellishment." — IVW (F)
- **Story through labour:** "in games it is the set of verbs, or what labor you can do, that tells the story." — [Cara Ellison, "The Gentle Verb"](https://playtime.pem.org/tag/cara-ellison/) (F)
- **Tone and characterisation through rules:** artgames' rules "characterize an idea … how it feels to experience or to be subjected to some sort of situation: marriage, mortality, regret." — Bogost, quoted at [Rhizome](https://old.rhizome.org/editorial/2009/jan/23/persuasive-games-the-proceduralist-style) (F)

### Finding and deepening a theme
- **Look outside games** (Schell Lens #11, F); "Draw inspiration from the real world"; "other media" (abagames, F).
- **Research the subject itself, then caricature it** (Wright: Forrester, ants, Alexander; "how does the game become a caricature of that subject", F). Rosenberg: "I'm always reading about the theme" (F, second-hand).
- **Ask why it excites you** (Schell Lens #10 Resonance; Wright's "What is the basis of my obsession?", both F).

### Joining theme and mechanics
- **Pasted-on theme is the failure case:** "the wholesale grafting of a theme onto a set of mechanics is dishonest if those mechanics have no real world connection to that theme. Can a game really be about exploring the Amazon if it can easily be re-themed to the terror of the French Revolution?" — quoted critic in [Juul](https://www.jesperjuul.net/ludologist/2004/08/11/should-game-mechanics-match-the-game-theme-aka-rules-and-fiction/) (F); "the theme is 'pasted on'" — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/how-to-marry-the-theme-and-mechanics-in-your-board-game) (F)
- **Ludonarrative dissonance** is the clash between what the story says and what the rules make you do. Clint Hocking coined it in 2007 about BioShock, which "promotes the theme of self-interest through its gameplay while promoting the opposing theme of selflessness through its narrative." **Disagreement:** Neil Druckmann (Uncharted 4): "we don't buy into it." — [Wikipedia, Ludonarrative dissonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludonarrative_dissonance) (F); Hocking's original post refused the fetch ([Click Nothing](https://clicknothing.typepad.com/click_nothing/2007/10/ludonarrative-d.html), S).
- **Fiction draws people in, rules hold them:** "fiction tends to govern one's initial interest in the game, but for the long term most people's interest might be held more strongly by the actual rules" (a commenter, Juul, F). Juul: "fiction matters, but in strange ways and with different degrees to different people" (F).
- **The unity test:** Schell's Unification and Tetrad lenses (F). Agreement among the four elements is a **Define** check, but Discover can gather which bricks pull together.

## 5. Verbs → mechanisms, nouns → interactables, and the short story

### Who describes Muzzy's move
- **Anna Anthropy, *A Game Design Vocabulary* ch. 2 "Verbs and Objects"** (via [First Person Scholar review](https://www.firstpersonscholar.com/?p=2351), F):
  - "verbs are the most important of all, as they are the rules that give the player the freedom to change the game state. The design of verbs affects both how a game feels and what it means."
  - "Good design is where verbs create interesting choices, and interact meaningfully with other verbs."
  - "Objects are exclusively those elements that are acted upon by a player's verbs, serving to complete the sentences the verb starts. Samus shoots a door, and it opens."
  - "Designers … should be wary of introducing too many objects or verbs. They should instead work on developing existing verbs and objects fully."
  - Naomi Clark adds "resources as rewards that enable player verbs, such as ammunition which switch on the player's ability to 'shoot'."
  - The reviewer's critique: "primary verb" is left undefined ("weird that we should say 'walking' is the primary verb in Mario … when the verb that seems to most obviously characterize that character is … 'jump'").
- **IVW framework ("IP → Verbs → World"):** start from a "You are…" line ("You are a caretaker for a rare breed of hydrangea-headed animals on the verge of extinction"). Verbs are "foundational actions"; "Skills" are "the intersection of two or more Verbs" ("Wall Climb" = Jump + Climb). "Think of the IP as your head in the clouds, and the verbs as your feet on the ground." "If your IP is well-defined but the verbs remain unclear, consider this a red flag." World entities each get traits, motivation and conflict. — [IVW](https://kebaux.bearblog.dev/ivw-game-ideation) (F). **This is the closest published match to Muzzy's process found.**
- **Cornell CS 3152:** theme first ("You will not worry about mechanics until you have defined your theme"), then goals, actions (verbs) and challenges; warns of "verb bloat". — (F)
- **Game design as a sentence:** "the subject is the Player, the verb is our core mechanic and the complement is the goal of the player"; "Adverbs allow you to extend the player experience" (jump → jump higher); "look at a list of action verbs and imagine what you want your player to do." — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-design-as-a-sentence-) (F)
- **Chris Crawford:** "The verbs are the content. Getting them right is the crux of the design problem." — [Siboot design diary](https://erasmatazz.com/library/design-diaries/design-diary-siboot/december-2014/21.html) (F). "Every interactive process provides the player … with a set of verbs" is **(snippet)** (search excerpt).
- **Koster:** verbs plus choice trees as the notation of a game (F, above). A Tartu thesis groups Koster, Crawford and Anthropy as verb theorists: "Verbs as the atoms of a formal game notation" — [Saks, U. Tartu](https://dspace.ut.ee/bitstreams/20e30cc8-bd40-4448-980c-c9e00caf6a98/download) (S, snippet).
- **Koster on the hard edge:** "Are the pieces in chess … verbs, or are they assets you manipulate? … when you go forward, they are verbs." — [Game Developer interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/defining-games-raph-koster-s-game-grammar) (F). The line between noun and verb blurs, so Discover should record both sides.

### Simulation thinking: nouns that have state, interacting under rules
- Systems: "Each 'part' in a system can be thought of an object … with its own state (defined by attributes) and behavior … The behavior of an object … may communicate with other objects, potentially changing their state." In MDA terms, "the mechanics … of a game system are its objects and their attributes"; dynamics "emerge as the result of game-based objects interacting." Example: dribbling was not in basketball's rules and was discovered later. — [Game Developer, "Systems, game systems, and systemic games"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/systems-game-systems-and-systemic-games) (F)
- Systems-based design: "standard player actions lead to complex game reactions." Cost: games that "open up the possibility space … tend to be much more difficult to accurately schedule." — [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/why-and-how-systems-based-game-design-works/) (F)
- Immersive sim: Spector's Deus Ex was "a game of problems (not puzzles!), all solvable in a variety of ways … create a believable world and then offer game systems that encouraged players to explore that world in whatever way or ways they chose." — [Deus Ex postmortem](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/postmortem-ion-storm-s-i-deus-ex-i-) (F)
- Harvey Smith's systemic-design talks ("Orthogonal Unit Design" 2003, "Systemic Level Design") are the likely primary sources for noun/verb unit design, but were only seen as titles **(snippet)**: [rpgcodex thread](https://rpgcodex.net/forums/threads/game-design-terminology-and-concepts-compiled-by-harvey-smith-in-2003.120304/post-5477719) (S), [witchboy.net](https://witchboy.net/?p=22) (S). Smith on why systemic play matters: "You're improvising and experimenting." — [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/the-designers-of-dishonored-bioshock-2-and-deus-ex-swap-stories-about-making-pcs-most-complex-games) (S, snippet).
- **Extrapolated:** the "simulation breakdown" step lists, for the theme, (a) nouns with their attributes (what state each holds: hot/cold, full/empty, trusting/suspicious), (b) verbs as outcomes, (c) a verb × noun grid of what each verb does to each noun, and (d) each living noun's motivation and conflict (IVW). Empty and surprising cells in the grid are where emergence and new mechanisms come from. That is the systemic version of Anthropy's "verbs … interact meaningfully with other verbs."

### Experience statements and pillars (the material Define writes the 3–5 line story from)
- Fullerton's experience goals (F) are one-sentence situations, not features.
- **Pillars:** "the 3 – 5 main elements/emotions your game is trying to explore," used to ask "does this mechanic/idea serve or fit into the pillars of our game?" — [Game Developer, Design Pillars](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/design-pillars-the-core-of-your-game) (F). That article's examples are mostly *features* (Crafting, Stealth). **Disagreement:** Tim Keenan's Duskers talk argues for pillars "focusing on emotions rather than features, refining the game's vision through experimentation, and focusing on player fantasy." — [GDC Vault, Finding Duskers](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024176/Finding-Duskers-Innovation-Through-Better) (F, session description only)
- Alexander Jaffe's "Player Mythologies" (GDC 2024) adds a second fantasy: the story players tell about themselves *as players* (explorer, collector). — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1034601/Player) (S, snippet)
- The one-sentence pitch test: "If you can't explain your idea in one sentence then you probably need to work out the problem." — [Game design as a sentence](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-design-as-a-sentence-) (F)
- **Not found:** no source uses Muzzy's exact "3–5 line story as the experience guide". The nearest are Fullerton's experience goals, IVW's "You are…" line, 3–5 pillars and the one-sentence pitch. **Extrapolated:** Muzzy's story is a narrative arrangement of experience goals plus fantasy plus the key verbs. Discover should therefore collect *candidate* experience goals, fantasies and verbs; it should not draft the story.

## 6. For BMUZ

### (a) Step shape: a creative discover run that collects bricks and never chooses

This keeps F217's pipeline (frame → questions → research with four lenses → hand-off card). The creative steps below set **what the frame asks and what the questions point at**. The four lenses (Mainstream, Extremes and failures, Outside the field, Disconfirm) stay as the *stance* each research helper takes. Steps 1–2 are talk; steps 3–6 are research topics the helpers split; step 7 is the hand-off. A small discover run (a feature, not a game) can skip steps that don't apply, and the frame says which.

| # | Step | Collects (bricks) | Heuristics / methods | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Spark** (talk) | The idea in Muzzy's words; a "You are…" line; what excites him and why; ready-made solutions put **on ice**; hopes and fears | Lens of Resonance; Wright's "basis of my obsession"; IVW "You are…"; H2 on ice, H25 hopes/fears | Schell (notes), AV Club, IVW, prior research |
| 2 | **Experience & fantasy** (talk) | Candidate experience goals (situations, not features); candidate core fantasy; MDA aesthetics in play; what players will tell friends | Fullerton experience goals; Essential Experience lens; MDA 8 aesthetics; Cornell "I want to __"; core fantasy | Fullerton, Schell, MDA, Cornell, game-design-cards |
| 3 | **Theme dig** (research) | What the subject really is (in and outside games): its systems, history, textures, tone, conflicts, what players already know about it | Wright subject research → caricature; Infinite Inspiration (outside games); Meier "player brings knowledge"; Rosenberg reading about the theme | AV Club, Tech Review, Schell, Meier, LoGM |
| 4 | **Simulation breakdown** (research + talk) | **Nouns** with attributes; **verbs** as outcomes; verb × noun grid; living nouns' motivation and conflict; skills = verb intersections; candidate decisions | Anthropy verbs/objects; Cornell outcome verbs; IVW entities; systems objects/attributes; Meier interesting decisions | FPS review, Cornell, IVW, Game Developer systems, Meier |
| 5 | **Mechanism shelf** (research) | For each outcome verb: 2–4 candidate mechanisms from catalogues, games that use them, and the skill each teaches; genre bundles that already cover some verbs | Designer-written catalogues (Building Blocks, pattern wiki); Koster atoms / "typical problems"; Cook skill atoms; "leverage genres" | Barker review, Koster, Cook, Cornell |
| 6 | **Prior art & twists** (research) | Close matches (what they share with us, praise, complaints, what they missed); possible hooks and anchors; twists (constraints, SCAMPER, strip-down, odd mashups, extremes) | Steam tag profile; competitive analysis; hook + anchor; SCAMPER; constraints; "first five ideas are too obvious" | Steamworks, Spector, Zukowski, abagames, jam article |
| 7 | **Brick board** (hand-off) | Every brick by shelf, its source and strength; on-ice ideas; open questions, each with a probe (a `/sketch` of the toy or core verb for feel; a `/proto` sim for numbers) | Toy lens / toy-first as **probes**, not choices; H22/H26 probes; prior research's merge rules (cluster, triangulate, keep contradictions) | Schell, Tiny Subversions, Northway, prior research |

Rules for the whole run (from prior research plus this one):
- Collect, never rank. The "choose" words (best, pick, should, recommend) belong to Define. Meier's "be ruthless in terms of cutting things out" is Define and Develop advice, not Discover's.
- Experience first or verbs first is Muzzy's call per run (sources disagree, section 1). Both shelves get filled either way.
- Verbs are written as outcomes, with the theme word alongside ("stitch → join two pieces into one"). The outcome form finds the mechanisms; the theme form keeps the fantasy. (Extrapolated from Cornell + IVW.)
- Keep the verb list short and developed in depth over long and thin (Anthropy; Cornell "verb bloat"). In Discover this means grouping duplicates, not cutting.
- Feel questions about real-time verbs go to a `/sketch` probe, not to reading (Swink; toy-first).

### (b) Question bank: 34 questions, a few at a time

Taste questions ask Muzzy's view first, before Claude offers anything. No engineering questions. Source in brackets; "extrapolated" means my inference.

**Step 1: Spark**
1. In one breath: what's the idea, in your own words? [prior research H6]
2. Finish this: "You are a…" Who or what is the player? [IVW]
3. What about this gets you excited? What's the bit you keep thinking about? [Schell Lens #10 Resonance]
4. Why this subject? What is it about it that you find really cool? [Wright, AV Club]
5. If there were no limits at all, what would this game be? [Schell Lens #10]
6. Any picture of how it plays already in your head? (Written down, kept, not chased yet.) [prior research H2 on ice]
7. What do you hope it becomes, and what would disappoint you? [prior research H25]

**Step 2: Experience & fantasy**
8. Describe a moment you hope a player has. Not a feature, a situation they find themselves in. [Fullerton]
9. After playing, what story would a player tell a friend? [Fullerton]
10. Which feelings is this about? (Show the 8 MDA words only after his answer: Sensation, Fantasy, Narrative, Challenge, Fellowship, Discovery, Expression, Submission.) [MDA]
11. What dream does it fulfil? "I want to ___." A role, an activity, or a feeling? [Cornell]
12. What questions should be in the player's head while they play? [Schell Lens #4 Curiosity]
13. Is it fun to *play with*, even with no goal? What's the toy in it? [Schell Lens #15 Toy]
14. What kind of player do you picture, and what do they believe about themselves as a player (explorer, collector, schemer)? [Jaffe, snippet]

**Step 3: Theme dig**
15. When you picture this world, what do you see, hear and touch? [Schell Tetrad, aesthetics; extrapolated]
16. What does everyone already know about this subject that the game can use for free? [Meier]
17. Is there an experience from your own life in this? [Schell Lens #11; abagames "your own experiences"]
18. Outside games, where does this feeling or activity show up: film, a sport, a job, a ritual, a toy? [Schell Lens #11; prior research H11]
19. What's the tone: cosy, tense, silly, sad, grand? [extrapolated]

**Step 4: Simulation breakdown**
20. What does the player *do* in this world? Say the verbs you'd use. [Anthropy; IVW; sentence article]
21. What things are in this world? Which of them does the player act on, and which act on their own? [Anthropy "Objects"; systems article]
22. For each thing: what state can it be in (full/empty, calm/angry, whole/broken)? [systems article, "state … attributes"]
23. For the living things: what do they want, and what stops them? [IVW motivation and conflict]
24. Which of your verbs are really one verb, and which combine into a skill? [IVW Skills; Cornell]
25. Where does the player have to choose, and what does each choice cost? [Meier]
26. What problems does the player solve here? [Schell Lens #6; Spector "problems not puzzles"]

**Step 5: Mechanism shelf** (Claude brings the catalogue results; Muzzy reacts)
27. Here are the outcome versions of your verbs ("stitch → join two pieces"). Do they still mean what you meant? [Cornell outcome verbs]
28. Which of these mechanisms feel like *your* verb, and which feel pasted on? [Juul/"pasted-on" theme]
29. Is there a genre whose verbs already cover part of this, so we only invent the new part? [Cornell "leverage genres"]

**Step 6: Prior art & twists**
30. Any games this reminds you of already? What do you love or hate in them? [abagames "analyze other games"]
31. Here are the closest matches. What did they miss that you'd want? [extrapolated from Zukowski hook + player complaints]
32. What's the familiar part a player would recognise (anchor), and what's the part they've never seen (hook)? [Zukowski; Hooks & Anchors]
33. What if we took something away (no shooting, one button, only cards)? Any limit that sounds fun? [abagames Restrictions; jam article]
34. What's the most extreme version of this idea? [prior research H12 extremes]

The 10 best (if a run can only ask ten): **2, 3, 4, 8, 9, 11, 13, 20, 21, 23**, plus **32** when prior art comes up.

### (c) What the research helpers look up, per step

| Step | Inside games | Outside games | Lens stance (prior research) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 Experience | Games known for that aesthetic or fantasy; how players describe them (reviews, forums) | Media and activities that produce the same feeling | Mainstream: how games usually deliver it · Disconfirm: evidence the target feeling doesn't come from this kind of play |
| 3 Theme | Games on the subject; what they kept and what they dropped | The subject itself: non-fiction, history, how the real system works (Wright's Forrester/ants), documentaries, hobbyist communities, image references | Outside the field is the main lens here |

### (d) The project-wide Discover document

One file per game (proposed path `.planning/DISCOVER.md`, next to the GDD). Two halves: shelves that accumulate across runs, and a log with one section per run. Extrapolated; the shape follows the step table.

```
# Discover — <game>
Seed: "You are …" · one-line idea in Muzzy's words · last run <date>

## Shelves (cumulative — every run adds; nothing is chosen here)
### Spark            — what excites, why (quotes from Muzzy, dated)
### Experience goals — candidate situations (not features) · MDA aesthetics in play · candidate fantasies
### Theme            — subject notes, references in/out of games, tone words, what players already know
### Nouns            — thing · attributes/states · acts or acted on · motivation/conflict (living things)
### Verbs            — theme word → outcome form · combines with → skill
### Verb × noun grid — what each verb does to each noun; empty/surprising cells flagged
### Decisions        — candidate choices and their tradeoffs
### Mechanism shelf  — outcome verb → candidate mechanisms → example games → skill it teaches
### Prior art        — game · shares which bricks · praised for · complaints/missed · its hook/anchor
### Hooks & anchors  — candidates only
### Twists           — constraints, SCAMPER moves, strip-downs, mashups, extremes
### On ice           — ready-made solutions and ideas held for Define (credited, dated)
### Open questions   — each with its probe (/sketch toy, /proto sim, ask Muzzy) and status
### Hopes & fears    — fears carried as assumptions with evidence status

## Runs (newest first)
### Run <n> — <date> — <topic>
Brief as said → goal underneath · steps run · research questions ·
findings (with strength marks + sources) · bricks added (links to shelves) ·
contradictions kept · surprises · full report: research/discover-<slug>.md
```

Rules: shelves are only ever added to or annotated, never trimmed, in Discover. Each brick carries its run number and source. Muzzy's own words are kept verbatim and marked as his (the global rule: his edits are decisions, never overwritten).

### (e) Clearly Define's job, not Discover's
- **Choosing bricks:** which experience goals become the 3–5 pillars, which fantasy, which verbs are core vs secondary, which nouns become interactables, which mechanisms win per verb. Pillar agreement uses Schell's Unification/Tetrad and Holographic lenses.
- **Writing the 3–5 line story** (Muzzy's experience guide) from the chosen goals, fantasy and verbs.
- **Picking the hook and anchor**, and the one-sentence pitch.
- **Judging:** Schell's Eight Filters ("novel enough?", "will it sell?"), Meier's cutting, ludonarrative fit checks, whether a theme is pasted on.
- **Scope per release**, GDD and TDD entries, the feature map. Engineering feasibility is the TDD's.
- Discover may *flag* tensions (e.g. "verb X and fantasy Y pull apart") but not resolve them.
