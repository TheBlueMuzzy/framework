BMUZ Design Document

This is the plan for how BMUZ takes a game from a loose idea to players' hands. It covers every stage and every step: what each step is for, what it asks you, how it gets its answer, and which part of the game's documents it fills. Comment on anything. Nothing here is built yet.

Built from: your stage decision (Ideate › Define › Design › Develop › Deliver › Release), the vision (V1–V48), the review of the Xong runs (33 questions, 15 of no use), 51 rework cases from Glyphtender, Goops and Roll Better, the design-method research (Fullerton, Schell, MDA, Ryan, Cerny, Meier and others) and today's live looks.

1 · The one big idea

One document, the Game Book (the GDD), with the same 16 chapters from the first minute to the launch. Every stage writes into those chapters, one level deeper than the stage before.

  • Ideate fills every chapter loosely: your words, your ideas, what's still open. Nothing is chosen.
  • Define chooses within the first eight chapters, the one-page concept.
  • Design works out the other chapters, tests the risky ones with sketches, then writes the build plan (the TDD and the feature map).
  • Develop marks chapters built as the game gets made, and keeps the book true.
  • Deliver and Release check the book against the finished game and the first players.

You always see the book as a page (the planner page, grown up): every chapter with its level, from grey to green. That's the "document I'm organising" link you asked for, at every stage.

Why this and not today's way
  • Today Ideate writes IDEATE.md with 9 headings, Discover wrote DISCOVER.md with 14 shelves, and Define writes GDD Part 1 with 8 others: three filing systems for one game. That's why Ideate's results didn't look like the start of the GDD.
  • The research agrees on loose → tight in passes: Cerny's macro design before micro, Fullerton's prototype right after brainstorming, Ryan's concept before the full spec.
  • Your words: "GDD › Project Planner Page › actually working through things", and the GDD as "a blueprint that you regularly update to be more and more reflective of the final product".

4 · What every step feels like

Every step opens like a page in a book, the way you described it:

Define · step 3 of 9 · The Pitch ● Frame ● Feelings ○ Pitch ○ Pillars … THE PITCH The game in a few lines, so anyone gets it in ten seconds. This becomes chapter 2 of your Game Book. We already have: "standard pong, but then things get weird" (your words). 1. In 3–5 lines, written to "you": what does a match feel like? e.g. Pong: "You're a paddle. The ball speeds up every hit…"
  • Title: the step's name, the same word as its chapter where it fills one.
  • What it's for: one or two sentences, shown once.
  • What we already have: your own words from the book, so nothing is asked twice.
  • The questions: 1–3 at a time, each with its context and an example from a well-known game.
  • The band under the chat names the stage, the step and which chapters are done.
  • The end of every stage is one card with a link to the Game Book page, what changed, and the one decision it needs.
Rules every question follows
  • Fixed questions (Where's your game? This pass?) are asked by BMUZ itself, instantly, without waiting 3–9 seconds for Claude to write them.
  • Never your first answer read back as options (Xong review: 6 of 33 questions did that).
  • Never a fiction question for an abstract game ("You are a…" for Pong).
  • Taste calls: your view first, then the best case for each option. Engineering calls: never asked; Claude decides and says so in plain words.
  • Links and long text: Claude asks in your words ("Share your links — paste them with Alt+V…") and your next message is the answer.
  • Several answers can be right? The question lets you pick several.

Research, any time

Research is a tool, not a stage: started when it's useful, never by default.

  • When: a creative block · a worry it's been done · something we've never built · a rule that doesn't work · before locking the concept (offered once).
  • How: sourced, every claim with a working link; independent helpers, merged.
  • Comes back as: "Here's what I found: does it change anything?", never a menu.

5 · How it's built

The parts that make the above happen the same way every time.

  • The Game Book is one file (GDD.md) with the 16 chapters as fixed headings. Each chapter carries its level (empty / loose / chosen / worked out / built) in a line BMUZ reads, so the page and the band can show progress.
  • Every step names the chapter it fills. A step's result is written into its chapter by a script, never by hand, so your edits and Obsidian edits are kept (V37).
  • The page is built from the book by a script and republished after every step: Game · Plan · Sketches · Open questions. Your planner page, grown up.
  • Each stage is a fixed list of steps held by the engine: you · Claude · script · helpers · gate. Claude thinks inside a step; any change of plan is logged where you can see it.
  • Fixed questions are asked by the engine (instant). Claude writes only the questions that depend on your game.
  • "Nobody's here" mode only switches on when the window really can't ask (a background run), never because Claude wrote a bad question. Today's Define ran without you for that reason.
  • What gets cut: IDEATE.md, DISCOVER.md, the 14 Discover shelves, Discover's theme dig, mechanism shelf and twists (now research on request). Raw reads stay in research/ as working files.

6 · The case against (and the answers)

Fixed chapters can make a loose idea look finished too early.

Answer: the level dots. A loose chapter is amber and says "loose", and open ones say "open: nothing yet". Ideate never fills a chapter with Claude's guess dressed as yours.

16 chapters is a lot for a small game.

Answer: a chapter the game doesn't need is marked "not in this game" in one tap (no opponents in a solo puzzle). The chapters come from real rework, so each one has cost a real game before.

Design docs barely predict success (Game Outcomes: under 0.2); risk management does (0.57).

Answer: that's why Risks & Tests is a chapter and Design starts by testing them. The book is the shared truth, not the goal.

More structure means more questions.

Answer: nothing already in the book is asked again, and every stage can wrap up early with the gaps marked. The Xong run had 33 questions; this plan targets under 15 for Ideate and 20 for Define.

Moving Design, Develop, Deliver and Release onto the engine is a big job.

Answer: it's done one stage at a time; until then each stage hands over to today's commands with the book as their input, so nothing breaks in between.
