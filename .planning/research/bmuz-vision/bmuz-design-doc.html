BMUZ Design Document

How BMUZ takes a game from a loose idea to players' hands: every stage, every step, the questions each step asks, how it gets its answers, and which part of the game's documents it fills. It's written for any game, so no step names a particular one.

What changed since draft 1 (from your notes)
  • The start is simpler. You describe your game, share links (read the moment you press Enter), and see the game so far. Where your game stands is worked out from those, never asked.
  • Define now runs high level to low: feelings › 3 pillars › the pitch › the core loop › platform.
  • Sketches are captured in Ideate and built in Design, and the planner page is introduced at the end of Ideate.
  • The rulebook is its own big step, in the structure of your own rulebooks. A content & balance sheet sits beside it.
  • Design splits into the game itself first, then the "meta game" (platform features, look, screens), which starts the TDD.
  • Teaching (tutorials) moves to Develop, after playtests.
  • Develop opens with setup and the roadmap. Deliver is a loop through playable builds, alpha, beta and 1.0. Release steps depend on the platform.
  • Research comes first at every step. Every stage, and every step, opens by teaching how it works.
  • There's a flow chart with the loops. Every step lists its real questions.

1 · The rules BMUZ lives by

  1. Research first. Before BMUZ suggests, builds or explains how something is done, it reads current sources and cites them, even for things it's done before. Its pick ("my pick") always rests on that research, never on memory. Research is offered as the first move at every stage, not saved for when you ask.
  2. Teach as you go. Every stage opens with a welcome: what the stage is for, how it flows, where you can step in and how BMUZ handles it. Every step opens with its title and a line or two on what it's for, like a page in a book.
  3. Ask like a guide who knows your game. Every question is built from what's already in the GDD: it quotes your words and asks about what's missing. Nothing already answered is asked again. A question that doesn't fit this game is skipped (a "who are you?" question for an abstract game, for example).
  4. High level to low. Big ideas first, specifics later: feelings › pillars › pitch › core loop › rulebook › content › screens.
  5. Expect loops. The rulebook will turn up new features, sketches and risks, so scope and risks get a first pass in Define and a real pass after the rulebook. The flow chart shows where things come back.
  6. Your taste, BMUZ's engineering. On taste (how it looks, feels, plays), BMUZ offers its researched take, and its view never overrides yours. Engineering calls are BMUZ's, explained in plain words.
  7. Show results, every step. Each step writes into the GDD, and you can always see the project page with the GDD's chapters filling in.

1.0

Welcome to BMUZ

once, at the very start

Welcome to BMUZ
I'll help you take a game from an idea to something people can play. We'll go through six stages, in order:

1. Ideate: gather everything about your idea. Nothing gets decided yet.
2. Define: decide what the game is, on one page.
3. Design: work out the rules, the content and the screens, and test the risky parts with quick sketches.
4. Develop: build it in short sprints you play and approve.
5. Deliver: turn each finished piece into a build you can play and share.
6. Release: put it in front of the public.

A few things to know:
· I research before I suggest anything, and I'll show you where my answers come from.
· Say anything, any time: an idea, a worry, "stop", "go back". A small tweak, I'll just do. A new idea goes on your Ideas for Later list so it's never lost. If something's broken, I log it as a bug.
· Everything we decide goes into one design document, the GDD. You'll watch it fill in on your project page.

Let's start with your idea.

Welcome to Define

teaches

Welcome to Define
In Ideate we gathered. In Define we decide, from the biggest ideas down to the specific ones:
1. The Feelings: how it should feel to play
2. The 3 Pillars: three rules that settle arguments later
3. The Pitch: the game in a few lines
4. The Core Loop: what you do, over and over
5. Platform & Play: where it's played, and who with
Then I check it all for clashes, we size up the scope, and you lock it.

Whatever your GDD already says shows up first, one tap to keep. Before each big decision I'll offer to research how other games handled it. Nothing here is final: the rulebook in Design will reopen some of this, and that's normal.
About 30–45 minutes. You can stop after any step; everything is saved.

The Feelings

The Feelings
The one or two feelings that are this game, and a sign we could actually see in a player. Every later decision gets checked against these.

  • ["moment 1"]
  • ["moment 2"]
  • [up to 4]

→ after their answer:

That sounds closest to [challenge]: [overcoming something hard and getting better at it]. Game designers often name eight kinds of fun: the senses, make-believe, drama, challenge, being together, discovery, self-expression, and simply passing time.

Is that the right match?

  • Yes, that's it

2.2

The 3 Pillars

you

The 3 Pillars
Pillars are three short rules about what this game is really about. Later, whenever we wonder "does this idea belong?", the pillars decide. Each is one to three words, plus a line on what it means here.
Two real examples:
· God of War (2018): Combat · Father & Son · Exploration
· Destiny: Enjoyable by the Impatient and Distracted · Shared with Other People

Want to see my take first?

  • Show me your take built from your feelings and the game so far
  • I'll write my own next, in your own words

→ "I'll write my own":

What are the three things [game name] is really about? One to three words each, and a line on what each means here.

→ then, for each pillar:

If we took away "[pillar]", would it still be [game name]? And finish this: "When we argue about ___, this pillar says ___."

→ a pillar that settles nothing:

"[pillar]" doesn't settle anything yet; it's more of a slogan. Want to sharpen it, or swap it for another?

teaches

Welcome to Design
The concept is locked. Now we work the game out in detail, in two halves.

The game itself
1. Sketches: we build the sketches on your list and play them
2. The Rulebook: the rules, written like a real rulebook
3. Content & Balance: every card, unit, ability and number, in tables
4. Change Over Time: what changes within a game and between games
5. Scope & Risks, second pass: the rulebook always adds things, so we size it up again

The digital game around it
6. Players & Opponents: the computer opponent, online play, saving
7. Look & Sound: the style, tested at real size
8. Screens & Menus: every screen and what's on it
9. The Build Plan: how it all gets built, and in what order

This is the longest stage, usually a few sittings. The rulebook is the heart of it and gets rewritten many times; that's how good rulebooks are made. You can stop after any step, and everything is saved.

3.2

The Rulebook

you · BMUZ drafts with you

The Rulebook
The rules, written like a real rulebook. This becomes the single truth: the game, the tests and the tutorial all follow it.

This is a first pass, so we capture every rule and don't polish. "TBD", "X" for a number we don't know yet, and two rival rules side by side ("Rule A / Rule B, to test") are all fine. It'll be rewritten many times, and a dated development log at the end records what changed and why.

The structure, the same order your own rulebooks use:
Overview · Components · How to Win · Setup · Gameplay (the Flow, then each action) · Game End · Scoring · Keywords · Development Log

5 · How it's built

  • The GDD is one file with fixed chapters. Each chapter carries its level in a line BMUZ reads, so the project page and the status band show progress.
  • Every step names the chapter it fills. Results are written in by a script, so your own edits (Obsidian, by hand) are kept.
  • The project page is rebuilt after every step from the documents: GDD (rulebook as a tab), content tables, sketches, roadmap, open questions, ideas for later.
  • Each stage is a fixed list of steps held by the BMUZ engine. Claude does the thinking inside each step, and any change of plan is logged where you can see it.
  • Fixed questions are asked by the engine instantly. Claude writes only the questions that depend on your game, from what the GDD already says.
  • "Nobody's here" mode only switches on for a real background run, never because Claude wrote a bad question.
  • Research is a standard step part: a sourced pass, every claim with its link, before each recommendation.
  • Cut: IDEATE.md, DISCOVER.md, the old Discover shelves and its 33 questions, the separate "where's your game?" questions.

6 · The case against (and the answers)

Fixed chapters can make a loose idea look finished too early.

The level dots answer it: a loose chapter is amber and says so, and an empty one says "open: nothing yet".

Pillars before the pitch is debated.

Some studios write the pitch first; Duskers found its pillars by experimenting. Your order (feelings › pillars › pitch) is kept, and the pillars may be revised after the first sketches.

Teaching late can mean a tacked-on tutorial.

Some studios start tutorials as soon as mechanics exist (Octodad). BMUZ keeps notes on what must be taught from Design onward, and builds the tutorial once playtests show where people get stuck.

Research first makes every step slower.

A sourced pass takes minutes and is offered before each big decision. Acting on out-of-date memory costs far more when it's wrong.

This is a big rebuild.

It's done one stage at a time with a live look by you between each, starting with today's five bugs. Until a stage is rebuilt, the current one keeps working.
