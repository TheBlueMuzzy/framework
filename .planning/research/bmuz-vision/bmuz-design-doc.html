BMUZ Design Document

How BMUZ takes a game from a loose idea to players' hands: every stage, every step, the questions each step asks, how it gets its answers, and which part of the game's documents it fills. It's written for any game, so no step names a particular one.

What changed since draft 1 (from your notes)
  • The start is simpler. You describe your game, share links (read the moment you press Enter), and see the game so far. Where your game stands is worked out from those, never asked.
  • Define now runs high level to low: feelings › 3 pillars › the pitch › the core loop › platform.
  • Sketches are captured in Ideate and built in Design, and the planner page is introduced at the end of Ideate.
  • The rulebook is its own big step, in the structure of your own rulebooks. A content & balance sheet sits beside it.
  • Design splits into the game itself first, then the "meta game" (platform features, look, screens), which starts the TDD.
  • Teaching (tutorials) moves to Develop, after playtests.
  • Develop opens with setup and the roadmap. Deliver is a loop through playable builds, alpha, beta and 1.0. Release steps depend on the platform.
  • Research comes first at every step. Every stage, and every step, opens by teaching how it works.
  • There's a flow chart with the loops. Every step lists its real questions.

1 · The rules BMUZ lives by

  1. Research first. Before BMUZ suggests, builds or explains how something is done, it reads current sources and cites them, even for things it's done before. Its pick ("my pick") always rests on that research, never on memory. Research is offered as the first move at every stage, not saved for when you ask.
  2. Teach as you go. Every stage opens with a welcome: what the stage is for, how it flows, where you can step in and how BMUZ handles it. Every step opens with its title and a line or two on what it's for, like a page in a book.
  3. Ask like a guide who knows your game. Every question is built from what's already in the GDD: it quotes your words and asks about what's missing. Nothing already answered is asked again. A question that doesn't fit this game is skipped (a "who are you?" question for an abstract game, for example).
  4. High level to low. Big ideas first, specifics later: feelings › pillars › pitch › core loop › rulebook › content › screens.
  5. Expect loops. The rulebook will turn up new features, sketches and risks, so scope and risks get a first pass in Define and a real pass after the rulebook. The flow chart shows where things come back.
  6. Your taste, BMUZ's engineering. On taste (how it looks, feels, plays), BMUZ offers its researched take, and its view never overrides yours. Engineering calls are BMUZ's, explained in plain words.
  7. Show results, every step. Each step writes into the GDD, and you can always see the project page with the GDD's chapters filling in.

1.0

Welcome to BMUZ

teaches

Shown once, at the very start.

Teaches
the six stages in one line each · that BMUZ researches before it suggests · that you can say anything, any time (an idea, a worry, "stop", "go back") and how it's handled: a tweak is done now, a new idea goes to Ideas for Later, a problem becomes a bug · that everything lands in the GDD you'll see on your project page
1.1

Your Idea

you · free text

The idea in your own words: the single most useful answer BMUZ gets.

Asks
Tell me about your game in your own words: what it is, what you love about it, anything you remember. Half an idea is fine. "I'm not sure yet" is fine too.
How
one free answer, no options: in the earlier live run, one free answer held most of the game by minute 3. Where your game stands (nothing yet / a concept / written / a prototype) is worked out from this answer and your material, never asked.
If unsure
"I'm not sure yet" leads to three spark questions, one at a time: What kind of game do you love playing lately? · A moment from a game, a film or your life you'd love to turn into play? · A theme, a mechanism or a feeling you keep coming back to? (Schell's Lens of Resonance; Fullerton's experience goals)
Fills
wherever it touches
1.2

Your Material

you · paste

Everything you already have, in one message. It's read the moment you press Enter.

Asks
Share your links — paste them with Alt+V. I can read Google Drive folder and/or doc links, GitHub links, and even folder and file links from your computer. If you don't want to share any links, just say "never mind".
How
read at once, the same way every time: Drive, GitHub and files on your computer are read only, and key or password files are skipped. Every line is sorted into the GDD's chapters; your own words are quoted and checked against the source.

1.5

Open Ideas

you · a few at a time

The half-formed parts of your idea, drawn out without choosing anything.

Teaches
BMUZ asks first about what's costliest to change later if it's guessed wrong. In past games, how and where the game is played, and the shape of the rules, caused the most rework (10 and 8 of 51 cases), so those come first. Look and polish come last.
Asks
gathering questions, never "pick one", each quoting a gap in your idea:
  • You mentioned [thing]: what ideas do you have for it, even half-formed?
  • Anything you remember about [part your material hints at]?
  • Where do you picture it being played: a phone, a computer, a store?
  • How does a game end, as far as you know?
  • What does a good player do that a new one doesn't? (Meier: a game is a series of interesting decisions)
  • What's the smallest version you'd want to play first?
  • What's already decided, and what's off the table?
How
2–3 short rounds; you can wrap up any time. "Not decided yet" is always an answer.

Sketch List

you · pick several

Things worth trying with a quick sketch, captured now and built in Design.

Teaches
a sketch is a small, throwaway playable page that answers one question: is this fun? which of two ways feels better? can you read it at phone size? It has knobs to tune, 3 presets and a "Copy values" button. The game is not built from sketches; they settle questions before building.
Asks
From what you've told me, these look worth sketching: [3–6 suggestions, each with the one question it answers]. Pick any. (several allowed) · then Anything else you're unsure of, or torn between two ways?
How
each sketch answers one named question, the riskiest first; rival options are built side by side (Schell's prototyping principles; Mark Brown; the Experimental Gameplay Project). Each pick becomes a numbered task: S01, S02…

2.1

The Feelings

you

The one or two feelings that are this game, and a sign we could watch for.

Asks
  • From the moments you gathered [shown], which one or two are this game?
  • In your words, what's the main feeling? then, after you answer, the eight kinds of fun in plain words (sense-pleasure, make-believe, drama, challenge, togetherness, discovery, self-expression, passing time) to see which yours matches (MDA)
  • Is the main reward beating something hard, being curious, being with people, or something that changes you? (Lazzaro's four keys)
  • How will we know a player feels it? What would you see them do? (Schell)
  • What would a game like this usually have that you deliberately don't want? ("non-goals", from design docs at Google and in GDD guides: only tempting things the game chooses not to do)
Why "won't"
listing what the game deliberately leaves out stops scope creep and settles later arguments. It's asked as a tempting thing to leave out, never as a negative goal.

2.2

The 3 Pillars

you

Three rules that will settle every "does this belong?" argument later.

Teaches
what a pillar is, with real examples: God of War's Combat, Father/Son and Exploration, and Destiny's "Enjoyable by the Impatient and Distracted". A pillar is one to three words plus a line of what it means.
Asks
  • What are the three things this game is really about? One to three words each.
  • For each: one line on what it means here.
  • If we removed [pillar], would it still be this game? (a pillar that fails this is a slogan, and BMUZ says so kindly)
  • For each: what argument would it settle? "When we argue about X, this pillar says Y."
How
Maine; Allgeier (3–5 short pillars); Koster's vision exercise; feelings first, so the pillars can grow from them. They may be revised after the first sketches (Duskers found its pillars by experimenting).

2.3

The Pitch

you · or BMUZ's take

The game in a few lines, so anyone gets it in ten seconds.

Asks
  • first: Want to see my take, built from your feelings and pillars, or write your own?
  • Who am I, what am I doing, and why is it cool? (Hacura's logline)
  • Describe a minute of play, written to "you". (Tim Ryan's concept document)
  • What's the one thing that makes it different from the nearest game you'd compare it to?
  • if you used "X meets Y": What exactly do you take from each? ("X meets Y" alone means different things to different people)
  • last: Read it back. Can you say it in one breath?
Research
offered first: Before we write it, want me to look at the closest games: what players love and miss, and what's new here?

The Core Loop

you

What players do over and over, in plain words first. The rulebook builds on this.

Asks
  • What does the player do most often, as one verb? (Unity)
  • Fill in: "You ___, then ___, so you can ___." (Unity)
  • What's the challenge, what do you do about it, and what do you get, and how does that set up the next challenge? (Brazie)
  • What happens in 3 seconds, in 30 seconds, in 3 minutes? and What's different the next time round? (Griesemer, Halo)
  • per verb: Is this fun on its own? (Ryan) · What things do you act on? (Anthropy's verbs and objects)
How
plain sentence first, diagram later; up to 3 core verbs (fewer and deeper beats many).

2.5

Platform & Play Structure

you

The big platform choices that change the design. The details wait for the TDD.

Asks
  • Where is the player, and what are they holding: a phone on a bus, a desk with a mouse, a couch with a controller?
  • Which one platform first (web, phone stores, Steam, itch), and which are "someday"? (one platform first: Ryan; course templates)
  • Who else is there: nobody, friends on the same screen, people online at the same time, or online taking turns days apart? (multiplayer is decided early: it touches everything)
  • If there's an opponent, must the computer be good enough to carry the game?
Later
match length, leaving and rejoining, saves and accounts go to step 3.5 and the TDD.

2.6

Clash Check

BMUZ, then you

BMUZ's critical read: what doesn't fit, what you might not see. Your game, your call.

How
separate reviewers read Part 1 against itself: do the pillars and feelings support each other? Does the pitch use the core loop? Is the theme only pasted on (Juul)? Are the decisions real trade-offs (Meier)? Is anything there only because it's cool (Thornton)? Each finding comes with its reason.
Asks
per clash: These pull against each other because [why]. Keep both, change one, or park one? BMUZ makes its case once and never digs in.

Scope, First Pass

you · charts

The big picture of what you'd build, and what a first playable slice looks like.

Teaches
MoSCoW: must / should / could / won't, with the test "if it were missing, would the game be pointless?" · Effort vs impact: four boxes (quick wins, big projects, fill-ins, avoid) · Vertical slice: a small piece of the game with a bit of everything that's core (gameplay, art, systems) at close to final quality. It's the first thing you'll be able to play and share.
Asks

(Derek Yu: small game first) · must / should / could / won't for each idea · then BMUZ places them on the effort–impact chart (effort is BMUZ's researched estimate, impact is yours) · Here's the slice I'd build first: does it feel like the game?
Shows
a first report: the chart, the slice, the risks, what needs research and which sketches (adds to the Sketch List). Marked "first pass": the rulebook will add a lot.

3.2

The Rulebook

you · BMUZ drafts with you

The rules, written like a real rulebook: the single truth the game, the tests and the tutorial follow.

Teaches
this is a first pass: capture all the rules, don't polish (Jesse Ross) · "TBD", "X", "[example]" and two rival rules side by side ("Rule A / Rule B — to test") are all fine · it will be rewritten many times, and the development log records why each change was made
Structure
the order of your own rulebooks (Blobimus, Sirius Smugglers, Dust Bunny Rumble, Glyphtender's Trial), which matches the published guides:
  • Title block: players · age · time
  • Overview · Components · How to Win · Setup (numbered)
  • Gameplay › Flow (the core loop as numbered steps) › one section per action or ability
  • Game End · Scoring · Keywords
  • Development Log: dated entries of Changed · Why · Result · To test · Ideas, as in your Killer Klowns rulebook, with a version tag (Law of Game Design's changelog)
Asks
core loop first (the Flow), then outward (Jesse Ross: start with core gameplay):
  • Walk me through one turn (or one moment), step by step.
  • How do you win, and how does a game end?
  • How is it set up?
  • What happens when [edge case BMUZ spots]?
  • What do players call each thing and each action? (one term per idea, kept the same everywhere)
  • What's secret, from whom, until when?
You read it
on your project page as a GDD tab, and you can edit it in Obsidian; your edits are kept.
Research
BMUZ reads published rulebooks of the closest games first, for how they structure similar rules.

Content & Balance

you · tables

Every card, unit, ability, level and number in tables: your second document beside the rulebook.

Structure
the shape of your own content sheets (Waveborn, Bytefall, Steampunch): one table per kind of thing, one row each: Name · Type · Cost · Effect · Flavour · Quantity · Balance value · a Playtesting table (date, players, start, end, duration, scores) · retired designs kept as "(deprecated)", never deleted
Asks

How
balance values from weighted formulas (Entro Games' spreadsheet guide); simulations or the math for odds; in the built game these tables are the files the game reads, so the Dev Kit can tune them live.

5 · How it's built

  • The GDD is one file with fixed chapters. Each chapter carries its level in a line BMUZ reads, so the project page and the status band show progress.
  • Every step names the chapter it fills. Results are written in by a script, so your own edits (Obsidian, by hand) are kept.
  • The project page is rebuilt after every step from the documents: GDD (rulebook as a tab), content tables, sketches, roadmap, open questions, ideas for later.
  • Each stage is a fixed list of steps held by the BMUZ engine. Claude does the thinking inside each step, and any change of plan is logged where you can see it.
  • Fixed questions are asked by the engine instantly. Claude writes only the questions that depend on your game, from what the GDD already says.
  • "Nobody's here" mode only switches on for a real background run, never because Claude wrote a bad question.
  • Research is a standard step part: a sourced pass, every claim with its link, before each recommendation.
  • Cut: IDEATE.md, DISCOVER.md, the old Discover shelves and its 33 questions, the separate "where's your game?" questions.

6 · The case against (and the answers)

Fixed chapters can make a loose idea look finished too early.

The level dots answer it: a loose chapter is amber and says so, and an empty one says "open: nothing yet".

Pillars before the pitch is debated.

Some studios write the pitch first; Duskers found its pillars by experimenting. Your order (feelings › pillars › pitch) is kept, and the pillars may be revised after the first sketches.

Teaching late can mean a tacked-on tutorial.

Some studios start tutorials as soon as mechanics exist (Octodad). BMUZ keeps notes on what must be taught from Design onward, and builds the tutorial once playtests show where people get stuck.

Research first makes every step slower.

A sourced pass takes minutes and is offered before each big decision. Acting on out-of-date memory costs far more when it's wrong.

This is a big rebuild.

It's done one stage at a time with a live look by you between each, starting with today's five bugs. Until a stage is rebuilt, the current one keeps working.
